Viral vs. Bacterial Infections: Which Medication Do I Take?

When it comes to taking medication, it's extremely important to take the correct one for your specific condition. One important factor to consider is what type of infection you have — taking the wrong type of medication won't treat it the way you want it to. While there are several other types of infections (more on those another time), the two most frequent are viral infections and bacterial infections.

Viral infections are caused by a virus, which the Cleveland Clinic describes as genetic information — DNA or RNA — inside a protective shell. There are many types, including respiratory, digestive-system, viral hemorrhagic fevers, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), exanthematous (rash-causing) infections, and neurological infections. Common examples include colds, the flu, hepatitis viruses, Ebola (which can damage blood vessels), and COVID-19. Viral infections spread through respiratory droplets, direct contact, contaminated surfaces or objects, food, water, or insect bites.

Bacterial infections, on the other hand, result from bacteria — single-celled organisms with genetic information encoded in a small piece of DNA. Common examples include food poisoning (gastroenteritis), skin, ear, or sinus infections, some STIs, bacterial pneumonia, most urinary tract infections (UTIs), strep throat, and E. coli. Like viral infections, they can spread by contact, vectors (bugs), vehicles (food or water), or through the air.

Both types of infection share similar symptoms — fever, body aches, fatigue, sore throat, cough, sneezing, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. That overlap is exactly why it matters to know which one you're dealing with: antibiotics work only against bacterial infections, not viral ones. In 2022, for example, azithromycin was among the most prescribed antibiotics, with roughly 8.8 million prescriptions issued — a drug meant for bacterial infections like bronchitis, pneumonia, and certain STDs, but sometimes reached for to treat viral illnesses like colds and the flu. Misuse like this is part of why resistance is growing in bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae. If you're dealing with a viral infection, an antiviral medication — matched to that specific illness (the flu, COVID-19, hepatitis B and C, HIV) — is the appropriate route. Depending on severity, blood transfusion or post-exposure prophylaxis may also be options.
